Summary

This was Pi Network's first Ecosystem Summit, an online event for the developers who build apps on Pi. The biggest theme was letting AI assistants do much of the work of building Pi apps: a new Developer Web Console and an MCP server let AI coding tools create apps and connect them to Pi. Pi also announced an App Growth Initiative with a budget of up to 20 million Pi to help apps attract users, and showed subscription payments and a payment method called x402 working on Testnet (Pi's test network). Also introduced: Pi DataLab, which connects identity-verified users with AI-related tasks; an AI skill that packages apps for SoloHost; a technical demo by the game company CiDi Games; and a conversation with the AI project Resonant.

  • The centerpiece was a new way of building apps with AI help. In the new Developer Web Console (a website where developers manage their Pi apps) and through an MCP server, an AI coding assistant can create and register an app, set it up, connect Pi sign-in (Pi Authentication) and Pi Payments, and apply to have the app listed. In a demo, founder Nicolas Kokkalis had an AI assistant build a multiplayer tic-tac-toe game from nothing, complete with notifications and payments (both charging players and refunding them).
  • The App Growth Initiative, paid for by the Pi Foundation, was announced with a budget of up to 20 million Pi. Apps that qualify can use part of that budget, based on their merit and performance, to attract and engage users who have completed KYC (identity verification). The exact rules for qualifying and the reward amounts will be announced later.
  • The Pi App Directory (the catalogue where users find Pi apps) was redesigned. Apps are still ranked mainly by Ecosystem Directory Staking — users staking (setting aside) Pi to support apps they like — and new sections were added: Bookmarked, Pi-Staked, Rising, Discover, and Pioneers Keep Coming Back.
  • Pi Launchpad (a platform for launching new tokens), a decentralized exchange (DEX, a marketplace for trading tokens with no central operator), and automated market maker (AMM, software that prices trades automatically) features will come to Mainnet (Pi's real, live network) in Q1 2027, including the first token launch on Mainnet through Launchpad.
  • Subscription payments run by smart contracts (programs stored on the blockchain that execute automatically) and x402 payments were demonstrated on Testnet. Subscriptions first appeared on Pi Testnet in April 2026. In the x402 demonstration, OpenMind's RoboPay paid 0.01 Test Pi to use a robot service.
  • A new platform called Pi DataLab was introduced. It connects Pioneers whose identity has been verified with small tasks that AI development needs, such as labeling data and judging AI outputs. Businesses and AI developers post these tasks, and Pioneers who complete them are paid in Pi or tokens. The first version is finished and running in a testing environment; a pilot version for real use is expected to pay people through their Mainnet wallets.
  • An AI skill for SoloHost was demonstrated. SoloHost lets people run apps on their own computers through Pi Desktop. Given the public Docker image of an existing app (a packaged copy of the app, ready to run), an AI agent writes the two configuration files needed to list the app on SoloHost and checks that they are correct. The demo used an AI agent app called OpenClaw and showed it being published and running on Pi Desktop.
  • A technical demo by CiDi Games showed how a game connects to Pi: Pi Authentication for sign-in, Pi Payments, the Pi Ads SDK (a toolkit for putting ads in apps), and local storage (saving data on the user's own device). The demo ran on a sample on a local computer, not on a live service, and CiDi stated plainly that its server does not yet verify users' access tokens (the digital passes issued at sign-in), a step the recommended procedure calls for.
  • Founder Chengdiao Fan talked with Jay of the AI project Resonant. They discussed Resonant, which checks AI decisions against human judgment; data as a productive resource (a basic raw material) of the AI economy; and the value of Pi's large base of identity-verified users.
  • Day 1 and Day 2 contained no new announcement on migration or KYC. Migration means moving users' Pi balances onto Mainnet. The number of fully KYC-verified users, over 18.1 million, was mentioned only as a figure for the size of the ecosystem.

About the Summit

The Pi Ecosystem Summit is Pi Network's first summit aimed at developers, held online from November 17 to 19, 2026. Day 1 (November 17) covered the developer platform, ecosystem initiatives (support programs for apps), and app showcases. The full Day 1 video premiered on Pi Network's official YouTube channel and runs 1 hour 45 minutes 5 seconds. Day 2 (November 18) covers AI and the ecosystem; Day 3 (November 19) kicks off the new hackathon "Pi Next" (a programming contest for building new apps), with a prize pool of 200,000 USDT (a digital currency tied to the US dollar).

In her opening remarks, founder Chengdiao Fan gave the ecosystem's scale: over 60 million Engaged Pioneers (active users), over 300 Mainnet apps, over 420,000 Nodes (computers running Pi's network software), and over 18.1 million fully KYC-verified users. These are the figures as officially stated.

Result 1: AI-assisted developer experience (Developer Web Console and MCP)

The main theme of Day 1 was a new way for developers to build apps, with AI assistants doing much of the work. Two things were introduced. The first is a new browser-based Developer Web Console (developers.minepi.com), a website where developers manage their apps. The second is an MCP server. MCP stands for Model Context Protocol; it is a standard way for AI programs to connect to outside tools, and Pi's MCP server lets an AI coding assistant use Pi's developer tools. Working from instructions in plain language, the assistant can create and register a Pi app, set up its settings and web addresses, connect Pi APIs (the connections apps use to talk to Pi) such as Pi Authentication and Pi Payments, and apply to have the app listed in the Pi app directory. By design, the assistant never sees secrets such as API keys (password-like codes that let programs identify themselves to each other) or wallet private keys, and a human must give authorization where it is needed. Developers who prefer the traditional way can do everything by hand in the same console.

In a demo by founder Nicolas Kokkalis, an AI assistant built a tic-tac-toe game ("Pocket Grid") from scratch. The steps shown were as follows.

  • The assistant built the game and deployed it to a public HTTPS URL (a public web address). At this stage it was an ordinary app with no Pi integration.
  • The assistant connected to Pi's MCP server and registered the app. It loaded the app in Pi Sandbox (a development environment that imitates the Pi Browser) and verified that it worked.
  • Multiplayer was added. One player creates a room and invites a friend through a PiNet link (a URL ending in pinet.com). Opening a PiNet link opens the app in the Pi Browser.
  • Notifications were added so a player is notified when their turn arrives. Communication between servers uses an API key, which the developer generates in the console and stores securely.
  • Pi Payments were added. Creating a room charges 1 Pi, with a refund if nobody joins. The demo showed both directions: user to app, and app back to user as a refund. This was on Testnet, and the app wallet's private key was kept by the developer, not given to the assistant.
  • Finally, the steps to submit the app for listing were shown. Much of the submission had already been prepared by the assistant.

The demo showed the multiplayer build taking about 10 minutes. The blog post likewise describes steps that used to take days now taking minutes.

Result 2: Pi DataLab (a new platform connecting human judgment with AI)

Pi DataLab is a new platform that matches organizations needing human judgment with Pi users around the world whose identity has been verified. AI development needs people to do work where quality depends on human understanding rather than automation alone: creating data, labeling it (for example, tagging what appears in a picture), evaluating AI outputs, ranking them, giving review feedback, and supplying physical AI data (real-world data used to train robots and similar systems). This kind of work is called "human-in-the-loop", because a human stays inside the process. On Pi DataLab, businesses, AI developers, and other organizations create and fund projects; participating Pioneers then voluntarily choose tasks, contribute their judgment or work, and receive Pi or tokens for completed contributions.

The demo used one example — classifying customer-support tickets by sentiment (positive, neutral, or negative) — and walked through everything from project creation to publication. The project, named "ticket sentiment", requested five responses per task. Participation requirements included KYC verification, language, and geography, and the project was published after Pi's review. Once live, clients can monitor metrics such as the average time to open and complete tasks and acceptance rates. On the task-doer's side, a task marketplace shows the payout and the estimated completion time for each task. Payouts are made monthly, according to the demo narration.

A quality layer records each task doer's reputation. Signals include genuine effort, time taken to open and complete tasks, submission attempts, and accuracy. These signals are used to maintain quality and to inform future task assignments.

The first version is complete and runs in a testing environment. The demo took place in that staging environment (a test setup that mirrors the real one), with payments disabled. The production pilot is expected to connect payments through Mainnet wallets. No release date was stated.

As background, Pi's KYC verification infrastructure has already coordinated more than 526 million validation tasks completed by over one million human validators, according to the blog post. The blog post also notes that rewards are paid in tokens including Pi, rather than in fiat currency (ordinary government-issued money) as on traditional Web2 platforms.

Result 3: AI-assisted SoloHost app packaging

SoloHost lets developers bring self-hosted applications (apps that would normally run on someone's own server) to Pi Desktop, opening a distribution channel among more than 420,000 Node operators and other Pi Desktop users. Any workload that can be packaged as a Docker image (a single package containing an app and everything it needs to run) can potentially run in SoloHost. A new AI skill — a set of instructions that teaches an AI assistant to do this packaging job — automates the work, and is provided in a public repository (github.com/pi-node/solohost). Given an existing application's public Docker image, a compatible AI agent (such as Claude Code, Cursor, or Codex) researches the application's capabilities, asks which settings to offer, generates the two required files (docker-compose.yml and config_options.yml), and validates them against SoloHost's validator (an automatic checker). The skill does not build the image itself.

The demo used the AI agent application OpenClaw as an example. The skill researched capabilities including three model providers (Anthropic, OpenAI, and OpenRouter), a Telegram connection, and DuckDuckGo web search; generated the two files reflecting only the capabilities the user selected; corrected an issue flagged by the hosted validator on its own; and ran a local test. Pasting the two generated files into SoloHost published the app, which then ran on Pi Desktop and answered a question about the weather in San Diego.

Result 4: On-chain subscriptions and x402 payments (demonstrated on Testnet)

On-chain subscription smart contracts

A smart contract is a program stored on the blockchain that runs automatically when its conditions are met; an on-chain subscription is a recurring payment handled by such a program. Pi's on-chain subscription smart contract lets developers build recurring payment services into their applications. It was first introduced on Pi Testnet in April 2026. The user approves a defined spending budget and subscription period in advance (for example, 6 months for a monthly subscription), without signing every renewal. Approved funds remain in the user's own wallet until each payment is processed, rather than being locked or transferred upfront. Users can extend or cancel their subscriptions. The contract data is managed through two records (the service offering and each user's subscription state) and remains on-chain for around 180 days.

The Day 1 demo created a service priced at 2 Pi per month (service ID 332), showed a user subscribing, and checked the subscription state in the wallet. In the demo example, the merchant could process the next cycle's charge between December 10 and January 10. Subscriptions are currently available only on Testnet. According to the blog post, the supporting infrastructure in Pi Wallet and the developer platform has now been implemented so both users and developers can use the full experience.

x402 payments integrated on Pi Testnet

x402 is an open payment protocol built on the HTTP 402 (Payment Required) response, the web's standard way of saying "payment is needed first". It works in steps: a server states the price, the asset, and the recipient; the client signs a payment approval and resends the request; and a facilitator (a helper service) verifies the approval and submits it to the chain. Funds move directly from payer to recipient in a single on-chain transaction, and the facilitator never holds the funds. The standard was created by Coinbase and is maintained by the x402 Foundation.

Pi has made x402 payments available on Pi Testnet. Day 1 featured a demonstration by OpenMind, together with the Fabric Foundation, of its RoboPay platform. A ping command to a robot (a short test message sent to check that it responds) was paid for with 0.01 Test Pi before the command was delivered, and only after the payment completed. The network fee was sponsored, so the payer's balance changed by exactly minus 0.01 Test Pi. Pi's network ID (pinet) has been merged into the Chain Agnostic registry (a shared registry of blockchain networks), and Pi's x402 payment specification has been submitted to the official x402 repository, where it is under review. The specification covers both Testnet and Mainnet, while details on Mainnet availability will be shared later. A Go SDK (a ready-made toolkit for the Go programming language) implementing the specification is also available.

Result 5: Ecosystem initiatives (growth support, app directory, Launchpad)

App Growth Initiative (up to 20 million Pi)

The "App Growth Initiative" is a new program sponsored by the Pi Foundation, announced with a marketing budget of up to 20 million Pi. Its purpose is to help apps grow without developers paying for the rewards themselves. Eligible developers can qualify for access to a portion of this budget based on the merits and performance of their applications, and use the Pi-funded incentive program to reach and engage KYC-verified users. As an example, when an eligible KYC-verified Pioneer invites another eligible KYC-verified Pioneer to use a participating app, both the referring Pioneer and the new user could each receive some Pi from the Foundation-sponsored incentive budget for qualifying participation. Exact eligibility requirements and reward amounts will be announced later.

Redesigned Pi App Directory

The Pi App Directory, the catalogue where users browse and open apps, was substantially redesigned. The existing "Ecosystem Directory Staking" mechanism, in which Pioneers stake Pi (set Pi aside as a show of support) to support apps and influence ranking, remains central. New discovery sections based on user interest, growth, and engagement were added.

Developer incentives are built from visibility, financial support, and status. As one financial example, Pi App Studio applies subsidized pricing (lower, partly paid-for prices) for creators whose apps demonstrate real utility and attract real users. Status is shown in the app directory, and strong apps may gain opportunities such as investment from Pi Network Ventures or candidacy for Launchpad.

Pi Launchpad, DEX, and AMM coming to Mainnet in Q1 2027

Pi Launchpad (a platform for launching new tokens), decentralized exchange (DEX, a marketplace where users trade tokens directly without a central operator), and automated market maker (AMM, software that sets trade prices automatically using pooled funds) capabilities will launch on Mainnet in Q1 2027. They have been on Testnet through multiple cycles of iteration (rounds of testing and improvement), and the launch includes the first Mainnet token launch through Launchpad. They will provide infrastructure for ecosystem token launches for specific utility purposes. The Q1 2027 timeline also gives potential Launchpad candidates, including participants in the upcoming Pi Next hackathon, time to build and prepare.

Other developer features

  • Improved PiNet deep linking: A PiNet link opens an app inside the Pi Browser. When someone opens a PiNet app link without Pi Browser installed, they are now prompted to download Pi Browser from the App Store or Google Play. After installing and opening Pi Browser, they are redirected to the original app or page they intended to visit, so an invited person can play a game session directly without manual navigation.
  • Notification Center: Developers can send users ecosystem notifications from their Pi apps, shown in one place inside the Pi Browser. These are in-app notifications, not push notifications to phones. During the initial rollout, applications can send notifications to up to 20 users without additional approval; notifying more users requires approval. Native push notifications are the next step, subject to more selective and restrictive rules to prevent spam.

These are the company's internal directions, however, and the token plan in particular was stated in the conversation as not yet discussed with the Pi Core Team. They are not confirmed announcements.

CiDi Games also described cooperation with the Pi Core Team, including a case in which players' KYC-related questions led to KYC survey questions being arranged and launched in less than two weeks. Fixes for iOS local-storage issues, a streamlined Mainnet verification process for new releases, and per-team advertising reporting were likewise made in response to developer feedback.

Fan described the broader strategy as connecting external demand to Pi's collective resources (distribution, attention, and user acquisition; human-in-the-loop work and data, with user consent; distributed computing; and identity). Gaming fits Pi well, she said, because payments run at scale on-chain, social interaction matches gaming, and games offer an easy on-ramp to financial participation. Stablecoin integration (connecting to digital currencies designed to hold a steady value) and further Web3 functionality are being considered within limits that do not compromise the central role of the Pi currency.

Following the conversation, a technical demo showed how the CiDi Games gaming platform integrates Pi's developer tools. It covered Pi Authentication (sign-in), Pi Payments, the Pi Ads SDK (Pi's toolkit for including ads in apps), and local storage (saving data in the user's own browser). The demo used a locally running sample to walk through the official SDK procedures, rather than a live production environment; this was stated repeatedly in the video.

For authentication, the demo requested the username and payment scopes (permissions), received Pi's authentication result (an access token, a temporary digital pass, and a user object), and linked it to an application account. It was explained that a Pi user identifier is specific to each application — not a universal identifier shared by all Pi apps — and that it may change when the user revokes permissions. Importantly, CiDi Games' current backend does not yet call Pi to verify the access token, and this was shown separately from the recommended procedure (sending the token to the backend and requesting the user resource from Pi to compare identifiers). It is not a claim that identity verification is performed in production.

For payments, the demo showed a top-up of the platform currency CDcoin: a 100-coin top-up, moving the balance from 101 to 201. It distinguished the approval, completion, and duplicate-protection stages, crediting coins only after completion is confirmed, and described a recovery flow that queries unfinished payments when a browser is closed mid-payment. The Pi amount shown was an illustrative quote in the sample, not a current exchange rate.

For ads, the demo used a rewarded advertisement (an ad watched in exchange for a reward) to make up a missed daily check-in. Watching the ad alone does not grant the reward: the backend queries Pi for the ad's status, and the reward is granted only when the mediator acknowledgement status is "granted". A pending, revoked, or failed status does not authorize the reward. Each ad identifier is accepted only once, preventing duplicate claims.

For local storage, the demo used a language preference (switching from English to Japanese) to show that a setting is saved in the browser and restored on relaunch. Storage is device-specific and does not guarantee cross-device synchronization or permanent storage. It was also stated in the video that CiDi Games' whitelist status for local storage access still needs confirmation.

The demo also described a shared game SDK and common verification service that lets multiple games reuse the same integration. Meme War and Ian Reap were named as examples. However, the spelling of both titles rests on the subtitles alone and could not be confirmed against official material.

Result 7: Developer conversation (Resonant) — human participation in the AI and data economy

Founder Chengdiao Fan spoke with Jay of the AI project Resonant. Resonant is a verification layer for AI decisions — a service that checks whether AI systems decide the way humans would. It collects human decisions on ethical dilemmas (for example, whom a domestic robot should save in a fire) and their metadata (information about those decisions), to measure whether AI agents decide the way humans would. About 1.7 million decisions have been collected so far, and Jay said the data becomes considerably more valuable at 15 million decisions (based on the spoken remarks alone).

The conversation covered data as an important productive resource of the AI economy, Pi DataLab as the link between that demand and Pioneers, AI agents distributed through SoloHost on Pi Desktop or as Pi apps, and the value of Pi's identity-verified user base in proving humanness and lowering onboarding barriers (the hurdles new users face when joining) for AI companies. Jay said Resonant chose Pi for its global user exposure and for one-click, identity-verified sign-in that lowers user-acquisition costs.

As advice for developers, Jay shared that writing an app in English only reaches about 40% of Pi's users, and that native translation increased user uptake two to three times (based on the spoken remarks alone). Pi's users speak nine languages, he said.

Resonant's future direction includes presenting questions in video form, collecting decision data in a way that is entertaining. Users hold their own decision profile — their own data — which they can link to agents and, in the future, connect to mechanisms for monetizing their data (earning money from it). Resonant is currently a non-profit research verification layer, and its metadata is published without personal detail.

Fan explained a plan to separate the Testnet and Mainnet distinction from the app-listing process. Listing in the app directory will be simplified as a single process, with the Testnet/Mainnet distinction applying only to operations involving wallets, such as payments.

The Resonant app in the Pi Browser was mentioned at the close of the video, but its exact URL could only be heard from the subtitles and is unconfirmed.
